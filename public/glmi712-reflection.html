Reflective AI Journaling: Serendipity, Sustainability Innovation and GreenLoop NZ

Ming Zhao
University of Auckland
GLMI712
June 2026

Introduction

This reflective journal examines my learning journey in GLMI712 through the development of GreenLoop NZ and through my use of AI as a thinking partner. GreenLoop started as a sustainability-driven innovation project, but it gradually became a personal learning experience about problem framing, technical responsibility, teamwork, contribution visibility, and the role of serendipity in opportunity creation. Before this course, I understood innovation in a practical way: if I could build a working product, then I had created value. Through this project, I realized that sustainable innovation is not only about launching a platform. It also requires understanding the system around a problem, noticing hidden relationships, communicating value to others, and reflecting honestly on both success and disappointment.

AI played an important role in this reflection, but not as a tool that simply generated answers for me. I used it to reframe my assumptions, challenge my interpretation of events, and connect personal experience with course concepts. In particular, AI helped me think more critically about the difference between “working hard” and “making work visible.” This distinction became important because my experience in the project was complicated. I contributed heavily to the technical side of GreenLoop, including the website, user flows, database thinking, and the practical structure of the platform. At the same time, I felt that the return I received, especially in evaluation and recognition, was much lower than the effort I invested. This was frustrating, but it also became one of the most meaningful parts of my learning.

Key Learnings from the Course

My first major learning was the importance of problem framing. At the beginning of GreenLoop, I saw the issue mainly as a waste problem. Students often throw away usable furniture, electronics, and household items when moving out, so it seemed natural to create a student second-hand exchange platform. However, after class discussions, project work, and AI-supported reflection, I started to understand that the real problem was broader. It was not only about waste. It was also about timing, trust, coordination, convenience, and student pressure. A student who wants to sell a desk may only have a few days before leaving accommodation. Another student may need the desk but may not see the listing at the right time or may not trust the seller. When these small failures happen together, a useful item becomes waste.

This changed my understanding of sustainability innovation. Many students care about saving money and reducing waste, but existing tools do not always support them when they are busy, stressed, or unfamiliar with local platforms. GreenLoop therefore became more than a listing website. I designed it as a student-first circular commerce platform that connected item exchange with email verification, reservation, chat, trust signals, delivery support, donation routing, community features, and job and resume matching. These functions came from the same systems-thinking idea: sustainability is easier when the surrounding process is convenient and trustworthy.

Kim (2023) argues that social innovation should focus on relationships between factors rather than isolated symptoms. This helped me move from asking “How do we stop students from wasting furniture?” to asking why exchange fails at the moving-out moment, why students hesitate to trust strangers, and why platforms become too slow under time pressure.

My second learning was that sustainable innovation is a process rather than a single moment of creativity. Belz and Binder (2017) describe sustainable entrepreneurship as a convergent process moving from problem recognition to opportunity identification and solution development. I experienced this directly. At first, I wanted to build quickly because I thought a complete website would prove the quality of the idea. Later, I realized that each function needed a reason. The reservation function responded to uncertain communication, student email verification responded to trust, delivery and donation functions responded to moving difficulties, and the job feature came from another observation: international students also need opportunities and support.

The most difficult learning was about contribution visibility. I put a very large amount of time into the implementation of GreenLoop. I stayed up late, debugged problems, structured user flows, and worked on making the website more complete and usable. In some weeks, I honestly felt that I carried much of the practical workload. However, the assessment and feedback I received did not seem to reflect that effort. I felt that I had given the most but received the least return. This feeling was not only about grades. It was about whether hidden technical labour could be recognized in an innovation course where presentation, attendance, and visible participation may be easier to evaluate than long hours of implementation work.

At first, I interpreted this mainly as unfairness. After using AI for reflection, I began to see it as a broader learning issue. Team-based innovation assessment can unintentionally make some forms of contribution more visible than others. A person who speaks more in class or presents clearly may appear more engaged, while someone who spends many hours building the actual product may become invisible if the process is not documented carefully. This does not mean teaching staff intentionally ignore technical work, but it suggests that assessment design should make room for different types of contribution. Individual process logs, GitHub evidence, peer contribution mapping, and milestone-based technical reflection could help teachers evaluate real effort more accurately.

AI-Influenced Insights

AI influenced my thinking in several important ways. The first was reframing. When I asked AI whether GreenLoop was mainly a waste-reduction idea, it suggested that I also examine the problem as a coordination and trust issue. This response challenged my initial framing. I began to see that sustainability behaviour depends on the surrounding system. If the platform is slow, confusing, or unsafe, students will not use it even if they support sustainability in theory.

The second way AI helped me was by challenging feature decisions. When I described GreenLoop as a platform with marketplace, chat, reservation, delivery, donation, community, and job functions, AI warned that the product could become too complex. I partly agreed. However, I also disagreed because my goal was not to create random features. I wanted to connect different student needs inside one practical ecosystem. This disagreement was useful because it forced me to explain why each function existed. AI did not simply give me an answer; it made me defend my own design logic more clearly.

The third way AI helped was more personal. I asked AI how to understand the feeling of working very hard but receiving little recognition. After I pushed it to analyze the situation from a team assessment perspective, it helped me name the issue as “invisible labour.” I realized that technical work can be central to a project but still difficult to see unless it is translated into evidence, reflection, and communication. I still believe the recognition I received was not proportional to my contribution, but I also learned that effort will not automatically be understood by others.

AI also helped me reflect on teaching and evaluation. I felt that some parts of the course assessment did not fully capture the complexity of project-based innovation work. The course encouraged creativity and sustainability thinking, but the evaluation process could have done more to distinguish between surface-level participation and deep project responsibility, especially when one student takes on a heavy technical role. AI helped me express this criticism more constructively. Instead of saying only that the result was unfair, I could ask: What evidence does the course need to evaluate contribution fairly, and how can builders, researchers, presenters, and coordinators all be recognized?

Serendipity and Personal Opportunity

The concept of serendipity was one of the most meaningful parts of the course for me. I used to think serendipity meant luck. Now I understand it as unexpected opportunity combined with preparation and action. Busch (2024) describes serendipity as a surprising discovery that becomes valuable through human action, and Fultz and Hmieleski (2021) connect it with discovering and exploiting unexpected opportunities. This theory fits my personal experience strongly.

Outside the course, I had been learning web development, building real projects, and experimenting with crawlers and database-related work. For a long time, these efforts did not bring immediate rewards. I often felt that I was working hard without being noticed. Then, because I had built a real website and shown practical ability, a business owner noticed my work and gave me a work opportunity. That moment felt lucky, but it was not pure luck. It happened because I had created visible evidence of my skills before the opportunity appeared. This helped me understand that serendipity can be designed indirectly. I cannot control who will notice my work, but I can increase the chance of being noticed by building real things, publishing evidence, and staying active in uncertain environments.

GreenLoop became part of this same process. It was a course project, but it was also a portfolio, a technical experiment, and a chance to show how I think about sustainability systems. Even though I felt disappointed by the assessment experience, the project still created value for me. It made my skills more visible and helped me connect sustainability innovation with my own development as a builder and problem solver.

Critical Evaluation of AI’s Role

AI was helpful, but its limitations were clear. The first limitation was that AI often gave safe and general suggestions. For example, when I asked how to improve GreenLoop, it recommended reviews, notifications, onboarding, and better search. These are reasonable, but they are also common platform ideas. They did not automatically understand the specific context of international students, moving pressure, trust problems, and campus culture. AI can process language quickly, but it does not truly understand lived experience unless I provide detailed context.

The second limitation was that AI followed my framing too easily. If I described GreenLoop as a waste problem, AI talked about sustainability awareness. If I described it as a marketplace, AI focused on transactions. If I described it as a fairness issue, AI focused on conflict resolution. This means AI can reinforce my own assumptions unless I deliberately ask it to challenge me. In future, I would use AI by asking for counterarguments, stakeholder perspectives, and uncomfortable questions rather than only asking for support.

The third limitation was that AI could not replace human judgement or emotional understanding. It could help me name “invisible labour,” but it could not fully understand the feeling of staying up late to build a project and then receiving less recognition than expected. It could help me write a more balanced criticism, but it could not decide what was fair. That judgement still required my own reflection, evidence, and maturity.

Personal Conclusion

Overall, this course and AI journaling practice changed how I understand innovation, serendipity, and my own role in project work. GreenLoop taught me that sustainability innovation is not only about environmental intention. It is about designing systems that make sustainable behaviour practical, trustworthy, and timely. AI helped me move from a simple product mindset to a more reflective systems mindset.

At the same time, the project taught me a difficult lesson about fairness and visibility. I still feel that my effort and return were not balanced. I believe I contributed more than was recognized, and I think the teaching and assessment process could better capture individual effort in team innovation projects. However, I also learned that this frustration can become useful if I turn it into better documentation, clearer communication, and stronger self-advocacy. In the future, I will not only build; I will also record why I built, what problems I solved, and how my work changed the project.

I would continue using AI as a reflection partner, not as a replacement for my thinking. I would use it to challenge assumptions, organize experience, and turn emotion into clearer analysis. This assignment helped me see AI not as a shortcut, but as a mirror and a sparring partner. Most importantly, it helped me understand that serendipity is not passive luck. It is created when preparation, visibility, and unexpected opportunity meet.
