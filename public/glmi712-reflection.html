Reflective AI Journaling: Serendipity, Sustainability Innovation and GreenLoop NZ

Ming Zhao
University of Auckland
GLMI712
June 2026

Introduction

This reflection examines my learning journey in GLMI712, especially how I used AI as a thinking partner while developing and reflecting on GreenLoop NZ. GreenLoop began as a sustainability innovation idea, but it also became a personal learning experience about problem framing, technical execution, teamwork, and unexpected opportunity. Before this course, I tended to understand innovation mainly as building something useful. Through the course, I realized that sustainable innovation is also about understanding the system around a problem, noticing hidden relationships, and being prepared to act when unexpected openings appear.

AI was involved in this process, but not as a replacement for my own thinking. I used it to ask better questions, reframe my assumptions, organize messy ideas, and challenge whether my solution was too technical or too narrow. Sometimes AI helped me see the GreenLoop problem from a system perspective. Sometimes it produced generic suggestions that I had to reject. This made the reflection more useful because I was not only evaluating my project, but also evaluating how I think when I use AI.

The most important concept I took from the course was serendipity. I used to think serendipity simply meant luck. Now I understand it more as the meeting point between unexpected events and purposeful action. Busch (2024) describes serendipity as an unexpected discovery that becomes valuable through human action, while Fultz and Hmieleski (2021) connect it with the ability to discover and use unexpected opportunities. This fits my experience strongly. I did not suddenly receive opportunities for no reason. Opportunities appeared because I kept learning, building, testing, and making my work visible.

Key Learnings from the Course

My first major learning was the importance of problem framing. At the beginning of GreenLoop, I framed the issue mainly as a waste problem. Students were throwing away usable items when moving out, so it seemed natural to build a platform for second-hand exchange. This framing was not wrong, but it was incomplete. After class discussions, user conversations, and AI-supported reflection, I began to see that the real problem was not just waste. It was also timing, trust, coordination, and student pressure. A student who wants to sell a desk may only have two days before moving. Another student may need the desk but cannot communicate quickly enough. A useful item then becomes waste, not because people do not care about sustainability, but because the system does not support the right interaction at the right time.

This shifted my thinking from a simple product idea to a broader system problem. GreenLoop therefore became more than a second-hand listing site. I designed it as a student-first circular commerce platform with email verification, seller trust signals, item reservation, chat, delivery requests, donation routing, service support, community posts, and job/resume matching. This taught me that sustainability innovation often requires connecting separate needs that are normally treated as different problems. In this case, environmental waste, student affordability, moving logistics, trust between strangers, and employment opportunities all existed in the same student life system.

This connects with systems thinking. Kim (2023) argues that social innovation problems should be understood through relationships rather than isolated symptoms. If I only focused on the symptom, I would ask, “How do we stop students wasting furniture?” But if I looked at relationships, I asked why students fail to exchange items in time, why they do not trust strangers, and why existing platforms are not convenient enough under moving pressure. These questions were more useful because they led to design decisions rather than moral judgement.

My second learning was that innovation is a process of convergence, not just a creative spark. Belz and Binder (2017) describe sustainable entrepreneurship as a process that moves from recognizing a problem to identifying opportunities and developing solutions. This matched my experience. In the early stage, I wanted to quickly prove that GreenLoop could work by building functions. Later, I understood that each function needed to answer a real problem. The reservation function responded to uncertainty when buyers and sellers chat for too long. Student email verification reduced trust friction by making the platform feel campus-based. The job and resume matching function came from another observation: students do not only need cheaper goods; they also need opportunities and support in a new environment.

My third learning was about personal agency. I often feel that I work hard but opportunities do not always appear equally. During the GreenLoop project, I spent a large amount of time writing the website, debugging the database, structuring the user flows, and making the product more complete. Because of this, I sometimes had to miss parts of class sessions or arrived with very little sleep. A difficult moment for me was feeling that this effort was not fully visible in the assessment process. I contributed heavily to the actual website and implementation, but the final recognition I received did not match how much work I felt I had done. At first, I felt this was unfair. After reflecting, I still think contribution visibility is a real problem in team-based innovation work, but I also learned that effort alone is not enough. I need to document my process more clearly, communicate my contribution earlier, and connect technical work with learning outcomes in a way that others can understand.

AI-Influenced Insights

AI influenced my thinking in several stages. In the first stage, I used AI to reframe the problem. My initial prompt was simple: “I am building a sustainability platform for students to exchange used items. Is this mainly a waste problem, or can it be understood in other ways?” The response suggested that the issue could also be seen as a trust, timing, and coordination problem. This was one of the most useful AI moments because it challenged my original wording. It did not give me the final answer, but it made me notice that my problem definition was too narrow.

In the second stage, I used AI to test feature ideas. I asked it to challenge whether a marketplace with many functions might become too complex. AI pointed out that students under moving pressure would not want a heavy platform. I still kept multiple modules in the MVP, but I tried to make each flow direct: list item, reserve, chat, arrange pickup, or request support. I agreed with AI that sustainability products should not ask users to do extra work just to be environmentally responsible.

In the third stage, AI helped me reflect on serendipity in my personal development. I asked AI: “How can serendipity be designed instead of just waited for?” The answer was that people can design conditions for serendipity by making their work visible, building weak ties, experimenting publicly, and staying ready to connect unrelated information. This connected with my own experience outside the course. I had been learning web development, building websites, and experimenting with crawlers and data-related tools. For a long time, these efforts did not seem to create immediate results. Then, because a real website I built became visible, an employer noticed my ability and gave me a work opportunity. That moment felt lucky, but it was not pure luck. It was preparation meeting an unexpected chance.

This changed how I understand serendipity. I cannot control when a person will notice my work, when a conversation will lead to an opportunity, or when a project will become useful beyond its original context. However, I can increase the chance of valuable accidents by continuing to build real things, sharing evidence of my work, and staying open to unexpected uses. GreenLoop itself became part of this process. It was a course project, but also a portfolio, a technical experiment, and a way to show how I think about systems and sustainability.

AI also helped me notice the emotional side of innovation. When I described my frustration about team contribution and assessment, AI first gave a generic answer about communication. After I pushed it to be more critical, it suggested that innovation teams often reward visible presentation work more easily than hidden implementation work. Technical labour can become invisible when the final product is simply shown as “the team website”. This helped me express the issue more maturely: not only as unfairness, but as a lesson about making invisible work visible.

Critical Evaluation of AI’s Role

AI was useful, but its limitations were also clear. The first limitation was that AI often produced safe and general answers. For example, when I asked how to improve GreenLoop, it suggested common platform features such as better search, user reviews, notifications, and onboarding. These ideas were not wrong, but they did not always respond to the specific context of University of Auckland students, international students, moving periods, or the trust issues in student-to-student exchange. This reminded me that AI is good at pattern recognition but weaker at understanding lived context unless I provide detailed information.

The second limitation was that AI followed my framing too easily. If I described GreenLoop as a waste-reduction platform, AI expanded on environmental awareness. If I described it as a circular commerce platform, AI talked about transactions and trust. This means AI can reinforce the direction of my own prompt. To use it well, I need to ask it to disagree with me, provide counterarguments, or analyze the same idea from different stakeholder perspectives. Otherwise, AI may make my existing assumptions sound more polished without actually improving them.

The third limitation was that AI could not replace human judgement. User trust, frustration, time pressure, and motivation are not only data points. They are human experiences. AI can summarize interview notes, but it cannot feel what it is like to move out of accommodation with only two days left, no car, and too many items to handle. It also cannot fully understand how discouraging it feels when a person works extremely hard but feels unseen by a team or assessor. These parts require direct experience, honest reflection, and conversation with real people.

However, I do not see these limitations as reasons to avoid AI. Instead, I see them as reasons to use AI more carefully. AI is strongest when I treat it as a sparring partner. It can help me generate options, organize information, and reveal blind spots, but I still need to decide what is relevant. In this sense, AI enhanced my creativity not by creating one amazing idea for me, but by slowing down my thinking. It made me ask: What problem am I really solving? Who is affected? What assumptions am I making? What would make this solution practical?

Personal Conclusion

Overall, this reflective journaling practice changed my understanding of innovation and serendipity. I started with the belief that building a functional product was the main achievement. I now see that the deeper achievement was learning how to frame a problem, connect different needs, and reflect on my own role in the process. GreenLoop taught me that sustainability innovation is not only about environmental values. It must also be convenient, trustworthy, and connected to real user pressures.

Serendipity became meaningful to me because it explained something I had experienced personally. Opportunities did not arrive simply because I hoped for them. They appeared when I had already created something visible enough for others to notice. This helped me feel less passive about the future. I cannot guarantee fairness in every course, team, or workplace, but I can improve how I document my contribution, communicate my value, and create more chances for unexpected connections.

I would continue using AI for reflection in the future. I would not use it to write my thinking for me, because that would make the reflection weaker. Instead, I would use it to ask better questions, test my assumptions, and compare different framings of a problem. This course helped me see AI not as a shortcut, but as a mirror. Sometimes the mirror is clear, sometimes distorted, but it still helps me notice what I might otherwise miss.
